What is Prompt Engineering?

Prompt engineering is the skill of crafting clear, effective instructions for AI tools. Think of it like giving directions to a very capable but literal assistant—the better your instructions, the better the results.

1 Be Specific, Not Vague

The clearer your request, the more accurate the output. Avoid general or open-ended phrasing.

❌ Vague Prompt
"Tell me about trucking insurance"
✅ Specific Prompt
"Explain the key differences between Motor Truck Cargo insurance and Truckers General Liability for a long-haul refrigerated freight operation in Texas."

2 Let the Facts Set the Role

Role-play preambles existed to pull the right expertise out of weaker models. State a real situation in real terms and the newest models already know what hat to wear.

❌ Then
"Act as a senior commercial auto underwriter with 15 years of experience in trucking risks."
✅ Now
"I'm underwriting a 35-unit refrigerated fleet and need to decide whether to pursue it." — the domain, the seniority, and the task are all implied by the facts.

Two places a role still earns its keep. When you want a genuinely different lens than the situation implies — "review this as the broker would," "argue the plaintiff's side." And as standing context: Anthropic still recommends setting a role in a system prompt, which for you means a Project's custom instructions, written once rather than retyped per message. What's dead weight is the per-message preamble that restates what your facts already say.

3 Provide Context First

Structure your prompt logically: background → rules/constraints → actual task.

✅ Layered Context
Background: "I'm reviewing a commercial auto policy renewal for a fleet of 15 power units operating in the Midwest."

Constraints: "The account has had 2 at-fault accidents in the past 18 months and their loss ratio is currently 72%."

Task: "What questions should I ask the broker to better understand their risk improvement efforts?"

4 Name the Deliverable, Not the Template

Format steering still works — it's dictated structure that costs you. A numbered output template caps the answer at the shape you already imagined; describing the job lets the model pick a better one.

❌ Then
"Provide: 1. Top 5 risk factors 2. Red flags 3. Information gaps 4. Appetite assessment. Use bullet points. Keep under 400 words."
✅ Now
"Give me your read — pursue, decline, or conditional — and what would change your answer. Lead with the call, then the reasoning. Keep it tight."

Still worth asking for

A real container: an email, a table, a one-pager, a letter. These are genuine constraints, not decoration.

Length controls

Keep these — the newest models write longer by default, so "keep it tight" or a word cap earns its place.

5 Describe the Standard, Not the Prohibitions

A stack of "do NOT" rules only fences off the bad without ever describing the good — and on newer models those rules actively compete with the judgment you're trying to use. One sentence about what good looks like does more work.

❌ Then
"Do NOT include specific financial details. Avoid technical jargon. Do not be pushy. Keep it under 200 words."
✅ Now
"Keep the reasoning at the level of the pattern rather than specific figures. Write it the way an underwriter writes to a broker they want to keep — firm, respectful, brief."

Hard rules still belong in a prompt when they're genuinely hard — a compliance line you cannot cross, a figure that must not leave the building. Say those once, plainly. The problem is the pile, not the principle.

The GRIP Framework for Effective Prompts

GRIP replaces CRISP. Every slot is information you supply rather than behavior you dictate — which is the whole difference between a prompt that helps the newest models and one that fights them. Four elements instead of five, on purpose: the shape of a framework should match its own advice.

Ground → Request → Intent → Proof

Get a grip on the real material first. Everything else is downstream of that.

G Ground

The real material. Attach the loss run, the ACORD, the report; paste the actual numbers. This is the highest-value part of any prompt and the one people skip.

✅ Example
"Three years of loss runs attached. 50-unit long-haul dry van fleet, 30+ states, mix of owner-operators and company drivers."

R Request

The decision you need made — and the container you need it in, if it matters. An email, a table, a one-pager. Not a numbered output template, which caps the answer at the shape you already imagined.

Strong Action Verbs

Analyze, Compare, Summarize, List, Explain, Create, Draft, Review, Identify, Calculate

Weak Verbs to Avoid

Help with, Tell me about, Do something with, Look at

✅ Example
"Tell me whether this risk is improving or deteriorating, and what loss pick you'd support at renewal."

I Intent

Why you need it, who it's for, what happens next. Anthropic's own guidance is that explaining the motivation measurably improves the answer — this is the most underused letter, and the one CRISP never had.

✅ Example
"This is going to the carrier on Thursday, so I need to defend the number rather than just state it."

P Proof

What you need marked: sourced, inferred, or unknown. The Reality Filter is this letter written out in full — for routine work, one sentence does it.

✅ Example
"Flag anything you're inferring rather than reading off the file, and tell me what would settle the open questions."

Coming from CRISP

Nothing you learned is wasted — three of CRISP's five letters survive under new names, one gets promoted, and one goes away.

Context → Ground + Intent

Split. The facts go in Ground; the why-and-who-for becomes its own letter, because it was the part everyone dropped.

Role → removed

A role that restates what your facts already say is dead weight. Still worth setting in a Project's standing instructions, or when you want a lens the situation doesn't imply.

Instruction → Request

Same job, sharper name: the decision you need made, not the artifact you want back.

Specifics → Ground

Promoted to first position, and the advice changed — attach the file rather than retyping a summary of it.

Preferences → Request + Proof

Split. The container (email, table) rides with the Request; the standard becomes Proof. The output template is gone.

The same prompt, before and after

In CRISP:

CONTEXT: I'm an underwriter reviewing a new business submission
for a 25-truck fleet that operates refrigerated goods across
the Southeast region.

INSTRUCTION: Identify the key risk factors I should investigate
further before quoting this account.

SPECIFICS: The fleet has been in operation for 3 years, has a
mix of owner-operators and company drivers, and reported
4 claims in the past 24 months (2 cargo, 1 physical damage,
1 auto liability).

PREFERENCES: Provide a prioritized list of 5-7 items with
a brief explanation of why each matters. Use bullet points.

In GRIP:

New business submission, 25-truck refrigerated fleet running
the Southeast. Submission and loss runs attached — 3 years in
operation, mix of owner-operators and company drivers, 4 claims
in the past 24 months (2 cargo, 1 PD, 1 AL).          [GROUND]

What should I be digging into before I quote this? Rank by what
would actually change the price, not by category.     [REQUEST]

I'm deciding whether to spend the week on it, so I care about
what would kill the deal more than what's merely
interesting.                                           [INTENT]

Flag anything you're inferring rather than reading off
the file.                                               [PROOF]
💡 The labels are for learning, not for typing
Don't put [GROUND] in your actual prompt — they're annotations to show you the four moves. A real GRIP prompt is just four short paragraphs of plain English. Note what got longer versus the CRISP version: the facts, and the reason. What got shorter: the instructions about format.

Zero-Shot vs. Few-Shot Prompting Beginner

Zero-shot: Ask directly without examples—relies on the AI's training.

Few-shot: Provide examples to guide the response format and quality.

✅ Few-Shot Example
Categorize these trucking operations by risk level:

Example 1: "Local delivery, under 100 miles" → Low Risk
Example 2: "Long-haul hazmat, 48 states" → High Risk
Example 3: "Regional refrigerated, 500-mile radius" → Medium Risk

Now categorize: "Intermodal container transport, port operations"

Chain-of-Thought (Step-by-Step) Intermediate

Ask the AI to think through problems step-by-step for better reasoning.

"Think through this step-by-step..." or "Walk me through your reasoning..."
✅ Example
"I need to determine if this account meets our underwriting guidelines. Think through this step-by-step: First, check the fleet size requirement. Then, verify the years in business. Next, assess the loss history. Finally, give me your recommendation with reasoning."

Dual-Pass / Self-Check Intermediate

Have the AI draft first, then refine against criteria. Great for important documents.

✅ Example
"Draft an email to a broker explaining why we're declining their submission. Then, review your draft and improve it to be more constructive while maintaining our position. Check that the tone is professional and includes clear next steps."

Multiple Viewpoints Intermediate

Ask the AI to consider different perspectives, then synthesize.

✅ Example
"Analyze this pricing decision from three perspectives: (1) the underwriter focused on profitability, (2) the broker focused on competitiveness, and (3) the insured focused on coverage value. Then recommend a balanced approach."

Prompt Chaining Advanced

Break complex tasks into a series of connected prompts, using each output as input for the next.

Step 1

"List all the risk factors in this submission..."

Step 2

"Now prioritize these by impact on loss potential..."

Step 3

"For the top 3, suggest mitigation questions..."

Step 4

"Draft my response to the broker..."

Request Validation Advanced

Ask the AI to check its own work for errors, gaps, or inconsistencies.

"Now review your response. Check for: logical inconsistencies, missing information, and potential errors. Correct anything you find."

Enforce "I Don't Know" Honesty

Tell the AI to admit uncertainty rather than making things up.

✅ Example
"If you're not certain about any regulatory requirements or specific coverage details, say 'I'm not certain about this—please verify' rather than guessing."

🔍 What is the Reality Filter?

The Reality Filter is a custom system prompt you can use to make AI responses more reliable, honest, and transparent. It forces the AI to:

📊 Separate What's Known

Sourced, inferred, or unknown - marked as it goes, not buried in a caveat at the end

🏷️ Say What Would Settle It

Not just "I'm unsure" but the specific document or number that would close the gap

🚫 No Invented Specifics

Never a number, date, name, or citation that isn't actually in front of it

🔎 Check First, Disclaim Second

With search available, looking it up beats declining to answer

📋 What Changed in v2

The original was written for models that would confidently invent things and rarely volunteer doubt. Newer models are better calibrated on their own, so the filter can do less - and several of its mechanics were working against it:

  • The 0.0-1.0 confidence score is gone. A self-reported "0.72" is a generated number, not a measurement - false precision from a directive built to stop false precision. Replaced with what actually helps: what would raise it.
  • Four labels became three, and one marks the good. [Inference] and [Pattern-Based] described the same act, and labelling only the doubtful parts left you unable to tell verified from merely unlabelled. [Sourced] fixes that.
  • The flagged-word list became a standard. Six banned words miss every overclaim phrased differently and trip on the legitimate uses.
  • Check before you disclaim. "I cannot verify this" was right when models had no tools. These can look things up.
  • Added a guard against over-verification. Newer models already re-check their work; a standing instruction that reads as "verify yourself" makes them do it twice and pad the answer. The filter now says it wants disclosure, not a second pass. The old self-correction clause was dropped for the same reason.
⚠️ If you already run v1
The labels changed. [Pattern-Based] folds into [Inference], [Speculation] is covered by [Inference] with its "say what from" showing the weak grounding, and [Sourced] is new. Update any saved Projects or custom instructions so your team reads one vocabulary.

🏷️ Required Labels for Unverified Content

The Reality Filter requires AI to prefix uncertain statements with clear labels:

[Sourced]

It's in the material in front of it - with a pointer to where

[Inference]

Concluded or projected - with what it was concluded from

[Unverified]

Not known - with what would actually settle it

✅ Properly Labeled Response
"[Sourced] Your submission lists 35 power units running refrigerated freight. [Inference] Reefer work at that size usually means produce or pharma - I'm reading that off the equipment and radius, not off a commodity list you've given me. [Unverified] Whether California adds endorsement requirements here I'd need to check against current DOT filings; tell me if they run CA and I'll look."

⚠️ Write Probabilistic Things Probabilistically

v1 carried a blocklist of six words. A blocklist misses every overclaim phrased differently and trips on the legitimate uses, so v2 states the standard instead - though these are still the words that most often signal the problem:

Prevent
Guarantee
Will never
Fixes
Eliminates
Ensures that
❌ Unacceptable
"Adding dash cams will prevent fraudulent claims and guarantees lower premiums."
✅ Properly Qualified
"[Inference] Fleets that add cameras generally see fewer disputed liability claims - the pattern is well established, but I'm applying it to your fleet, not measuring it. [Unverified] The premium impact turns on the carrier's telematics credit, which isn't in anything you've sent me. Nothing here supports guarantees: the direction is likely, the size isn't knowable from this."

⚖️ Pros/Cons for Brainstorming

When brainstorming or exploring options, the Reality Filter encourages forward-thinking analysis:

✅ Brainstorming Output Format
Question: Should we expand into hazmat trucking coverage?

PROS:
  • [Sourced] Higher premium per unit - industry standard, widely published
  • [Inference] Less competition in specialty markets, reading off the barrier to entry
  • [Sourced] Diversification of the book
CONS:
  • [Sourced] Higher severity potential
  • [Sourced] Specialized underwriting expertise required
  • [Unverified] Regulatory complexity varies by state - I'd need the target states to say how much
Forward-Looking:
  • [Inference] EV adoption may change hazmat transport dynamics over 5-10 years - projecting from current fleet-electrification trends, not from anything specific to hazmat
  • [Inference] Training investment would be needed, based on the team's current expertise

📋 Copy-Paste: The Complete Reality Filter Prompt

Add this to your system prompt or paste at the beginning of important conversations:

REALITY FILTER

Ground every claim in what I've given you or what you can actually
check. Keep three states visibly separate as you go:

[Sourced]      It's in the material in front of you - say where.
[Inference]    You concluded or projected it - say what from.
[Unverified]   You don't know - say so, and what would settle it.

Never produce a number, date, name, quote, or citation that isn't in
front of you. A plausible-looking figure is worse than "I don't have
that" - I can act on the second one.

Write probabilistic things probabilistically. Words like guarantees,
eliminates, will prevent, or ensures need a source that actually says
so. Otherwise tell me what's likely, and how strongly.

If you can look something up, do that before telling me you can't
verify it. If you still can't, say which it is: you didn't check, or
you checked and it isn't there.

This is a disclosure instruction, not a verification one - mark what
you know as you write. Don't re-audit finished work to satisfy it.

💡 When to Use the Reality Filter

✅ Great For

Anything that gets quoted, priced, filed, or acted on - regulatory questions, coverage interpretations, risk analysis, underwriting decisions

⚡ Skip For

Drafting an email, tidying notes, general explanations - the labels are just noise there, and it isn't free

📌 Where to Put It

A Claude Project's custom instructions or a Cowork workspace, so every conversation inherits it - not retyped at the top of each chat

💡 Pro Tip: Combine with the GRIP Framework

For maximum effectiveness, pair the Reality Filter with GRIP — in fact Proof, GRIP's fourth letter, is the Reality Filter in one sentence. Put the filter in your Project's standing instructions, then structure each question as Ground → Request → Intent → Proof.

🚚 Underwriting Use Cases

Practical prompts for common underwriting tasks. Remember: Never include real PII—use generic descriptions instead.

Submission Analysis

CONTEXT: I'm reviewing a new business submission for 
commercial trucking insurance.

TASK: Based on the following account characteristics, 
identify key risk factors and questions I should ask:

- Fleet size: 18 power units
- Operation: Regional LTL, 400-mile radius
- Years in business: 5
- Commodities: General freight, some refrigerated
- Loss history: 3 claims in 36 months (1 large cargo claim)
- Driver tenure: Mix of experienced and newer drivers

OUTPUT: Provide a prioritized list of 5-7 risk factors 
and 3-4 specific questions for the broker.

Coverage Comparison

TASK: Create a comparison of Motor Truck Cargo coverage 
versus Truckers General Liability for a client who is 
confused about the difference.

FORMAT: Use a simple two-column comparison covering:
- What it covers
- What it doesn't cover
- When claims typically occur
- Key coverage limits to consider

Keep explanations simple enough for a new broker 
to understand.

Loss Run Analysis

CONTEXT: I have loss run data showing claim patterns 
for a trucking account seeking renewal.

TASK: Help me analyze these claim characteristics and 
identify trends:

Claim patterns (generalized):
- 2 backing accidents in parking lots (minor PD)
- 1 rear-end collision on highway (moderate BI)
- 1 cargo shortage claim
- 1 weather-related accident

Questions to answer:
1. What patterns do you see?
2. What driver training might help?
3. What questions should I ask about their 
   safety program?
4. Any red flags for renewal?

Broker Communication

TASK: Draft an email to a broker explaining that we 
need additional information before we can quote 
their submission.

TONE: Professional, helpful, not demanding

NEEDED ITEMS:
- Updated driver list with MVRs
- Current vehicle schedule
- Explanation of the large cargo claim from last year
- Safety program documentation

CONSTRAINTS: Keep under 200 words. Be specific about 
what we need and why. Include a reasonable deadline.

Regulatory Quick Reference

TASK: Summarize the key insurance requirements for 
commercial trucking operations, including:

1. Federal minimum liability limits by operation type
2. Cargo insurance requirements
3. Common state-specific variations
4. Filing requirements (BMC-91, etc.)

FORMAT: Organized by topic with bullet points.

NOTE: Flag anything that may have changed recently 
or varies significantly by state—I'll verify those 
details separately.

Training / Knowledge Building

TASK: Explain the concept of "radius of operation" 
and why it matters for underwriting trucking risks.

INCLUDE:
- Definition and how it's typically classified
- Why it impacts risk and pricing
- Common misrepresentations to watch for
- Questions to ask to verify actual operations

AUDIENCE: New underwriter with 6 months of experience

FORMAT: Conversational explanation with practical examples

⚠️ CRITICAL: Protecting Private Information

AI tools process data on external servers. Never input actual personally identifiable information (PII) or sensitive business data.

❌ Real names
❌ DOT numbers
❌ SSN / EIN
❌ Driver's licenses
❌ Policy numbers
❌ VIN numbers
❌ Actual addresses
❌ Phone numbers
❌ Email addresses
❌ Bank accounts
❌ Claim numbers
❌ MC numbers

✅ Safe Alternatives

Use generic descriptions instead of real data:

❌ Don't Write
"Review the submission for ABC Trucking LLC, DOT# 1234567, located at 123 Main St, Chicago IL"
✅ Do Write
"Review a submission for a mid-size trucking company based in the Midwest with 5 years in operation"
❌ Don't Write
"Driver John Smith, DOB 01/15/1980, CDL# D123456789, has 3 violations..."
✅ Do Write
"A driver with 15 years experience has 3 moving violations in 36 months including..."

The Generalization Technique

Convert specific data to categorical descriptions:

Specific → General Pattern:

"ABC Trucking" → "a regional carrier"
"Chicago, IL" → "a major Midwest metro area"
"47 trucks" → "a medium-sized fleet (40-60 units)"
"$2.3M in claims" → "claims exceeding $2M"
"John Smith" → "the primary driver" or "Driver A"

⚠️ When in Doubt, Leave It Out

If you're unsure whether information is sensitive, err on the side of caution and use a generic description instead. The AI doesn't need real identifiers to provide useful analysis.

Safe Data Categories

These types of generalized information are typically safe to share:

  • Operation type (long-haul, regional, local)
  • Commodity categories (dry van, refrigerated, flatbed)
  • Fleet size ranges
  • General geographic regions
  • Years in business
  • Claim counts and general patterns (not specifics)
  • Coverage types and limit ranges
  • Industry benchmarks and standards
  • General regulatory requirements

📌 Prompt Starter Templates

Copy and customize these for common tasks. They're written the GRIP way — plain paragraphs, no ALL-CAPS section labels. The bracketed notes tell you what belongs where; delete them as you fill each one in.

General Analysis

[The situation, plus the real material — attach the file
where you have one, and paste the actual numbers.]

[The decision you need made, and the container you need it
in if it matters: an email, a table, a one-pager.]

[Why you need it, who it's for, what happens next.]

Flag anything you're inferring rather than reading off what
I gave you, and say what would settle the open questions.

Document Drafting

[The situation, and the facts the document has to carry.]

Draft a [type of document] for [audience].

[Why it's going out and what it needs to achieve — this is
what lets the model judge tone better than you can specify
it.]

[What good looks like, in one sentence: "the way an
underwriter writes to a broker they want to keep — firm,
respectful, brief."]

Learning / Explanation

[What you already know about the topic, and where you got
stuck — the gap is the most useful thing you can give.]

Explain [concept] to me.

I need it because [what you're about to do with it], and I'm
coming at it as someone who [experience level].

Where the answer depends on something you can't see from
here, say so rather than picking a case.

🎯 Quick Tips Checklist

  • Start with context before making requests
  • Assign a specific role to shape expertise
  • Use action verbs (analyze, compare, draft)
  • Specify your desired output format
  • Set boundaries on what to avoid
  • Ask for step-by-step reasoning on complex tasks
  • Request the AI validate its own response
  • Tell it to admit uncertainty vs. guessing
  • Never include real PII—use generic descriptions
  • Iterate and refine—follow up for better results

❌ Too Vague

"Help me with this submission"

✅ Specific

"Identify the top 5 risk factors in this fleet submission"

❌ No Context

"What questions should I ask?"

✅ With Context

"For a new trucking account with high driver turnover, what questions should I ask about their safety program?"

❌ No Format

"Tell me about cargo insurance"

✅ Clear Format

"Summarize cargo insurance in 5 bullet points suitable for explaining to a new broker"
